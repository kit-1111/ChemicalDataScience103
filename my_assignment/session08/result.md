The data set contains 13 chemical features with 178 observations and these are classified into 3 classes. The number of class 0, class 1 and class 2 are 59, 71 and 48 respectively. The descriptive statistics show substantial differences in the scales and distributions of the features. For example, alcohol has a mean of approximately 13.00 and a standard deviation of 0.81, while proline has a mean of approximately 746.89 and a standard deviation of 314.91.
The pairplot was selected for some features including "alcohol", "ash", "magnesium" and "flavanoids". The most noticeable separation appears in the distribution of flavanoids. Class 2 generally has much lower flavanoid values than Class 0, while Class 1 tends to occupy an intermediate range. This suggests that flavanoid concentration could be an important predictor of wine class. The pairplot also suggests that magnesium and ash provide less obvious separation when examined individually. Their distributions overlap considerably across the three classes. This does not mean that these variables are useless; they may still contribute when combined with other chemical measurements.
There are no missing values or duplicated rows, but some features contain observations at the extremes of their distributions. For example, malic acid ranges from 0.74 to 5.80, magnesium from 70 to 162, and color intensity from 1.28 to 13.00. The maximum color intensity is notably higher than its upper quartile of 6.20. Similarly, the ash minimum of 1.36 is relatively low compared with its lower quartile of 2.21. These values may represent potential outliers.
The correlation heatmap reveals several strong relationships between chemical measurements. The strongest positive correlation is between total phenols and flavanoids, with a correlation coefficient of approximately 0.865. Also, negative correlation is shown in some cases, such as colour intensity and hue.
According to the PCA visualization, PC1 explains around 36.2% of the total variance and PC2 explains around 19.2%. With two components, 55.4% of the total variance is explained. The PCA scatterplot shows three distinguishable groups. Class 0 is concentrated mainly on the right-hand side of the plot. Class 2 is mainly concentrated in the upper-left region, whereas Class 1 is concentrated in the lower and central regions. The separation is clear while some are overlapped. This suggests that the chemical features contain information that distinguishes the wine classes and that the differences involve combinations of variables rather than a single feature alone.
Results from three different models are higher than those under PCA. This may possibly due the 55.4% of total variance is tested. Logistic Regression performs best among the PCA models, reaching 91.67% test accuracy. The Decision Tree and Random Forest both achieve 88.89%. Notably, the train accuracy of decision tree is 100% in both cases, but the accuracy under PCA is lower.

Does PCA reduce dimensionality without substantially reducing classification accuracy?
Hypothesis: Reducing the dataset to two principal components will simplify visualization but reduce classification accuracy compared with using all 13 features.
Justification: The first two principal components explain approximately 55.4% of the variance. The supplied results show lower test accuracy after PCA for all three models: Logistic Regression drops from 97.22% to 91.67%, while Decision Tree and Random Forest drop from 97.22% and 100.00%, respectively, to 88.89%.

Can alcohol concentration alone reliably distinguish the three wine classes?
Hypothesis: Alcohol concentration contains useful class-related information but is insufficient to classify every sample accurately on its own.
Justification: The violin plot shows differences between class medians: Class 0 has the highest median alcohol concentration, Class 1 the lowest, and Class 2 an intermediate value. However, the distributions overlap. Therefore, samples from different classes can have similar alcohol concentrations.
